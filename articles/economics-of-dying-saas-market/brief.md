## Reader and problem

Primary audience: AI-curious leaders. Secondary audience: startup operators.

They are paying for a SaaS stack whose real cost they cannot see. The problem they arrive with, in
the idea's terms: "SaaS doesn't just charge you money — it charges you cognitive overhead." They
need a decision framework they "can actually apply this to evaluate their own SaaS stack."

## Why now

The economic calculus has shifted because AI collapsed build costs. The timing evidence the idea
records: $285B evaporated from software stocks in the first week of February 2026 (IGV ETF -23%),
Goldman Sachs compared it to the structural decline of newspapers; 35% of Retool's surveyed
customers have already replaced at least one SaaS tool and 78% expect to build more in 2026; Garry
Tan says 25% of the current YC batch has 95% AI-written code.

## Provisional thesis

Provisional. From the idea's Thesis Statement:

> The economic calculus of SaaS has flipped: the average company spends $4,830/employee/year on SaaS
> tools, with true costs 3-5x the license price — but the real killer is the conformance tax nobody
> measures. AI has collapsed build costs to the point where a custom app hosted on Railway for
> $5-25/month can replace a $50/seat/month SaaS tool that was never quite right. The sweet spot
> isn't "build everything yourself" — it's AI-built custom software hosted cheaply: purpose-built
> tools that conform to your workflow instead of the reverse.

The angle is a two-sided ledger — obvious build costs on one side, the hidden conformance tax on the
other — and the decision framework in one line: "When the cost to build drops below the conformance
tax, SaaS stops being a bargain."

## Original contribution

From the idea's "The User's Unique Contribution":

1. **Living the transition in real-time.** Fractional CTO across 3 clients, each with different SaaS
   stacks (Jira, ClickUp, Zoho, Harvest, etc.). Sees the conformance tax multiplied across
   workspaces daily.
2. **Built the replacement.** The knowledge worker framework is the case study — markdown-based task
   management replacing Jira's ontology; morning briefings replacing the 45-minute tab-opening
   ritual across 10+ SaaS tools; a SOC2 compliance kit replacing Vanta/Drata.
3. **Coined "conformance tax."** Nobody else has named this concept; a sticky label makes it
   measurable and actionable.
4. **Can show real numbers.** The Claw piece published the "before"; this piece provides the economic
   framework for "why" and the hosting economics for "how."
5. **Isn't a zealot.** Former GDPO who understands compliance requirements and can credibly draw the
   line at payroll, regulated industries, and deep-domain tools.

The competitive gap the idea states: "Nobody has combined (a) concrete SaaS cost data, (b) a named
concept for the hidden cost (conformance tax), (c) real hosting economics for the alternative, and
(d) a practitioner case study showing the transition — all in one piece with a decision framework
readers can apply to their own stack."

## Scope

Long-form Substack piece (~2,200-2,500 words) that leads with concrete economics — "here's what SaaS
actually costs" — names and defines the conformance tax, adds the integration tax that compounds it,
shows what changed on the build side of the ledger, then positions AI-built custom software hosted
cheaply as the emerging middle category, with a comparison table and a stated kill zone.

## Exclusions

The idea's stated limits: the argument "isn't anti-SaaS or pro-DIY" and is not a "replace all SaaS"
case. Multi-state payroll, regulatory compliance engines, and deep-domain vertical SaaS are
acknowledged as out of the kill zone rather than examined. The kill zone is confined to horizontal
tools — project management, simple CRM for a small team, internal dashboards, workflow automation,
approval flows.

## Reader takeaway

The decision framework in one sentence: if you spend more time conforming to the tool than the tool
saves you, the economics have flipped. The close the idea plans: "The best tool is the one that
thinks like you do," and the invitation — look at your SaaS stack, find the tool you complain about
most, that is your first candidate.
