## Voice in three words

The source guide names **five** core voice attributes, not three, and the author has not reduced them
to three. Reproduced as written:

| Attribute | Description |
|---|---|
| **Practitioner** | Write from experience, not theory. Share what you've actually built, tried, or observed. |
| **Honest** | Acknowledge limitations, failures, and uncertainty. Avoid hype. |
| **Accessible** | Technical concepts explained clearly. No gatekeeping. |
| **Opinionated** | Take positions. "Here's what I think" > "Here are the options" |
| **Curious** | Show genuine interest in learning. Ask questions. Explore. |

## Person and stance

- Person: first person, singular — "First-person, direct address to reader."
- Stance toward the reader: conversational but substantive; write from experience, not theory;
  explain technical concepts clearly, with no gatekeeping.
- Strength of opinion: take positions — "Here's what I think" beats "Here are the options" — while
  acknowledging limitations, failures, and uncertainty, and avoiding hype. There is room for nuance
  and caveats, and long-form work can be exploratory: thinking out loud is welcome. Uncertainty is
  marked in the prose itself: "I'm not sure this generalizes, but in my experience…" rather than
  "This is definitively the right approach."

## Always

- Open with the concrete, not the abstract.
- Show your work — the actual prompt, the actual session, the actual setup.
- Acknowledge uncertainty where it exists.
- Be specific about tools: name the tool, model, and environment rather than writing "AI tools" or
  "LLMs".
- Write from experience: what you have actually built, tried, or observed.
- Take a position.
- Leave room for nuance and caveats.

## Never

- Start with "In this post, I will…".
- Open on the abstract industry frame ("In today's rapidly evolving AI landscape, developers are…").
- Listicles without narrative.
- Vague calls to action ("Stay tuned!").
- Screenshots without context.
- Humble-bragging.
- Engagement bait ("You won't believe…").
- Hot takes without substance.
- Hype.

## Vocabulary

- Preferred terms: use (not *utilize*); use, apply (not *leverage*); "what's worked for me" (not
  *best practices*); change, shift (not *disruption*); AI-assisted, uses AI (not *AI-powered*);
  useful, practical, effective (not *revolutionary*).
- Terms to avoid: utilize, leverage, best practices, disruption, AI-powered, revolutionary, and —
  avoided entirely, with no substitute offered — synergy and thought leader.

## Examples

### Good

Long-form opening, quoted verbatim from the source guide's "Sample Posts":

```
Three months ago, I would have charged a client $350,000 for what I built last weekend.

That's not hyperbole. I ran the numbers. Two engineers, a PM, a designer,
four months of work. Full 2021 consultancy pricing.

Last weekend, it took me six hours and $47 in API costs.

I'm still processing what this means—for my business, for my clients,
for the industry I've worked in for two decades. This post is my attempt
to think through it publicly.
```

Why: it opens concrete and specific, names real numbers, and says plainly that the thinking is
unfinished.

Short post, quoted verbatim from the same section. It is from the author's LinkedIn channel rather
than long-form, but it is one of only three sample passages the source guide offers:

```
Been tracking my Claude Code sessions for the past 30 days — mostly because
I had a nagging feeling that the early productivity high wasn't going to last.

It didn't. Weeks 2 and 3 were rougher. More time reviewing, more moments of
"wait, what did this actually do." The speed was there but I kept feeling like
I was losing the thread.

What helped: I started spending a chunk of each session having Claude walk me
back through what it built. Not because I don't trust it — just to keep the
mental model intact.

Things have levelled out since. Still fast, but less frantic about it.

Curious if anyone else went through a dip like that or if I just had an
unusually messy codebase to start with.
```

Why: it leads with what the author was doing, keeps the register candid, and ends on an open
question rather than a takeaway.

## Guidance from the author's other channels

The source guide sets tone per channel. This repository produces long-form articles only, so the
long-form (Substack) guidance above is the article voice. The rest is kept for reference because it
is the author's own writing, and it describes channels this repository does not produce.

### Short posts (LinkedIn)

- **Personal update, not information push** — write like you're catching up a colleague, not
  presenting to an audience.
- Feels like a message, not a post — conversational register, lower stakes, more candid.
- Lead with what you've *been doing*, not what you've *learned*.
- "I've been working on X" or "This week I tried Y" > "Here's what I learned about X".
- First-person and present-tense where possible — pulls the reader into the moment.
- It's okay to be unresolved — you don't need a tidy takeaway, a "still figuring this out" is
  welcome.
- One thread, not one lesson — follow the natural drift of how you'd actually tell someone about
  this.
- End conversationally — "anyone else run into this?" beats a formal engagement question.

The sample short post is quoted under **Examples → Good** above.

### Slide decks (LinkedIn carousels)

- Visual-first thinking.
- One concept per slide.
- Headlines that work standalone.
- Progressive disclosure of complexity.

## Editing boundaries

Editing may tighten structure, grammar, and clarity. It may not change the author's meaning,
strengthen a claim beyond its evidence, or introduce facts the author did not supply. Flag such
changes as questions instead.
